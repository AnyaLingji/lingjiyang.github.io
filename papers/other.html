Toward the Other

Theory, Morality, and Body
Advanced Study of Anthropological TheoryAnthropological Theory · Ethics · Embodiment · Human–AI Interaction

Lingji Yang · 2025

01 / Overview

What does it mean to encounter another person, culture, or form of existence without reducing it to our own assumptions?

Coming from a background in communication studies, I have long been interested in how one consciousness can meaningfully connect with another.

Martin Buber's philosophy of I and Thou offers an ideal of encounter in which the other is approached as a subject rather than an object or instrument.

Yet encountering others also requires recognising the limits of one's own understanding, the power involved in representing different worlds, and the ethical responsibilities that accompany observation.

This reflective learning diary explores these questions through anthropological theory.

Organised around three dimensions of encounter, Theory, Morality, and Body, it reflects on how anthropological perspectives challenge established ideas about knowledge, ethics, embodiment, and human relationships.

The final section connects these insights to my developing research interests in AI-mediated intimacy, power, and subjectivity.

02 / The Theory of Encountering the Other

From Evolutionism to Cultural Relativism

The diary begins by tracing major intellectual traditions in anthropology.

Early evolutionary approaches attempted to organise societies into universal stages of development. Although these theories sometimes acknowledged shared human capacities, they frequently reproduced hierarchical distinctions between Western observers and the societies they studied.

The work of Franz Boas challenged such universalising accounts by emphasising historical particularity, cultural relativism, and the importance of understanding cultural phenomena within their own contexts.

Malinowski's commitment to understanding people's perspectives further established ethnographic engagement as a fundamental anthropological practice.

Culture, Structure, and the Problem of Representation

Subsequent theoretical traditions developed different accounts of how human experience and social life should be understood.

The culture-and-personality school emphasised the relationship between cultural patterns and individual development.

Structural functionalism directed attention toward observable social relations, while structuralism explored the underlying symbolic and conceptual structures shaping human thought.

These traditions raised an enduring question: who has the authority to describe and interpret another person's world?

Beyond the Self–Other Divide

Drawing on Lila Abu-Lughod and Nancy Scheper-Hughes, the diary examines how the concept of culture can sometimes reinforce artificial distinctions or obscure unequal power relations.

The ontological turn provides another challenge to conventional representation by asking anthropologists to take seriously the different kinds of worlds their interlocutors inhabit.

Through these debates, I came to understand anthropological inquiry as an encounter in which questions of knowledge and existence remain closely connected.

The effort to understand others also requires reconsidering the assumptions through which we understand ourselves.

03 / The Morality of Encountering the Other

Objectivity, Intervention, and Ethical Responsibility

The second part addresses the tension between the researcher's responsibility to describe social realities and the moral responsibility to respond to suffering.

The diary begins with the ethical controversy surrounding Kevin Carter's photograph The Vulture and the Little Girl.

The image raises difficult questions about observation, intervention, professional responsibility, and the moral judgments directed toward those who document suffering.

The Debate over Objectivity

Roy D'Andrade's defence of objective description provides one perspective on this dilemma.

From this position, research should prioritise accurate accounts of social phenomena rather than allowing moral judgment to determine interpretation.

Nancy Scheper-Hughes offers a contrasting argument, emphasising the ethical responsibility of anthropologists to recognise suffering, violence, and unequal power relations.

The diary considers this tension without treating either position as a universal solution.

Morality in Context

Drawing on Joel Robbins, Hollan and Throop, and Michel Foucault, the discussion moves toward an understanding of morality as historically and socially situated.

Moral life involves attempts to pursue the good, respond to suffering, and negotiate conflicting obligations within particular circumstances.

Returning to Carter's photograph, the diary considers how public condemnation can simplify complex situations by assigning individual blame while obscuring structural violence.

The central reflection is that ethical understanding requires attention to context, competing perspectives, and the limits of immediate moral judgment.

Anthropological inquiry does not always provide definitive answers, but it can deepen our capacity to engage with moral complexity.

04 / The Body of Encountering the Other

Technology and Sensory Reconfiguration

The third part turns toward embodiment and technological mediation.

Inspired partly by Cyberpunk: Edgerunners, the discussion asks how technologies transform the relationship between the body, perception, and identity.

Communication technologies mediate sensory experiences unevenly.

Highly developed visual and auditory technologies can produce immersive experiences, while the absence or limited development of touch, taste, and other sensory dimensions introduces new forms of incompleteness.

Drawing on anthropological discussions of time–space compression and hybrid bodies, the diary considers how technologically mediated perception may transform memory, experience, and the sense of a unified self.

Hybrid Embodiment and Subjectivity

Technologies increasingly participate in the ways people perceive, represent, and construct themselves.

Digital environments allow individuals to experiment with identities, social roles, and alternative forms of self-presentation.

These possibilities complicate assumptions that identity is a stable inner essence waiting to be discovered.

Instead, technological mediation makes visible how subjectivity can be continually shaped through social, material, and symbolic practices.

Can AI Have a Body?

The final discussion raises a more fundamental question: can an artificial intelligence robot with a physical form be understood as possessing a body in an anthropological sense?

Drawing on Michael Jackson, Thomas Csordas, and anthropological approaches to embodiment, the diary examines the distinction between a body understood as an instrument and embodiment understood as the foundation of lived experience.

AI robots complicate this distinction.

Although their sensors and physical components allow them to interact with the world, their design remains closely connected to instrumental and economic purposes.

The diary therefore questions whether attributing a body to AI merely on the basis of physical functionality would reproduce the instrumental logic that theories of embodiment seek to challenge.

This reflection leads to a broader question about the kinds of worlds humans wish to inhabit and the forms of human and nonhuman embodiment with which they may coexist.

05 / Connecting Anthropology to AI Intimacy

Technologies of the Self and Digital Discipline

The final part of the learning diary connects anthropological theory with my developing master's research on AI-mediated BDSM interactions.

Drawing on Michel Foucault's theories of disciplinary power and technologies of the self, I explore how interactions with AI may transform conventional understandings of power and submission.

When an AI performs a dominant role within an interaction, its behaviour remains partly shaped by the user's instructions.

This creates a paradox in which the experience of being controlled may also involve the user's active organisation, clarification, and direction of desire.

From this perspective, AI-mediated role-play can be understood as a potential practice of digital self-discipline and self-fashioning.

Desire, Embodiment, and Becoming

The diary also draws on Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari's concepts of desire-production and the Body without Organs.

These perspectives offer a way to consider desire as something produced and transformed through interaction rather than simply expressed as a pre-existing preference.

In digital environments, desires are translated into prompts, categories, descriptions, and interactional scripts.

Language and technical systems become part of the process through which embodied and emotional experiences are imagined, organised, and explored.

AI as an Experimental Space

The diary considers AI-mediated role-play as a potentially reversible environment for exploring power, identity, and desire.

Such interactions raise questions about the relationship between human agency and technological responsiveness.

They also challenge familiar distinctions between self and other, physical and digital embodiment, and control and surrender.

These reflections formed part of the early theoretical exploration that preceded my completed master's thesis.

They represent developing research questions and conceptual possibilities rather than findings from an independent empirical study.

06 / Final Reflection

Anthropological theory has expanded my understanding of communication as an encounter with others.

The course introduced intellectual traditions that question how difference is represented, how moral judgments are formed, and how bodies and subjectivities are shaped through social and technological relations.

Across its three central themes, the learning diary returns to a shared concern: understanding others requires recognising the limits of our own perspectives.

This reflection also informed my growing interest in human–AI interaction.

As technologies increasingly mediate intimacy, embodiment, and self-understanding, questions of otherness and encounter extend beyond conventional interpersonal relationships.

For me, anthropology offers valuable tools for approaching these questions with theoretical curiosity, ethical sensitivity, and attention to the complexity of lived experience.
