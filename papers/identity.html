Identity, Cognitive Consistency, and Intergroup Tolerance

A Research Plan on Identity Structures and Responses to Conflicting Group Norms
Social Psychological Foundations of Intergroup ConflictResearch plan · experimental design

Lingji Yang · 2025

A research plan on how identity structures may shape conformity, independent judgment, and tolerance when personal experience conflicts with group norms.

01 / OVERVIEW

Why do people sometimes change their personal opinions just because their group disagrees with them?

Imagine meeting someone from another social group. You have a pleasant conversation and form a positive impression.

Later, you discover that most people in your own group have a negative opinion of this kind of person.

What happens next?

You might begin to doubt your first impression and agree with your group. Or you might continue trusting your own experience and question why your group thinks differently.

This research plan explores what makes people respond differently to the same conflict.

My central idea is that the way people organise their social identities may influence whether they follow group norms or maintain their own judgments.

To investigate this question, I propose a survey and a two-stage experiment set within the context of gender-polarised online discussions in China.

02 / RESEARCH QUESTION

When personal experience conflicts with group norms, why do some people follow their group while others maintain their own judgments?

I focus on two possible responses:

Response A: Norm Conformity

A person initially likes someone but changes their evaluation after learning that their own group disapproves of this person.

They adjust their personal judgment to become more consistent with the group's position.

Response B: Independent Reconsideration

A person initially likes someone and maintains that positive judgment despite learning about the group's negative opinion.

They may also begin to question whether the group's judgment is reasonable.

The research asks whether these different responses are related to how individuals organise their social identities.

03 / THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

Cognitive Consistency

Cognitive consistency theory suggests that people generally prefer their beliefs, attitudes, and self-understandings to fit together.

When two important beliefs contradict each other, people may experience psychological discomfort and attempt to resolve the contradiction.

In this study, the conflict involves two judgments:

  • Personal judgment: “Based on my experience, I think this person is good.”
  • Group judgment: “People in my group think this kind of person is bad.”

Both judgments may matter to the same individual. The question is which one they are more willing to reconsider.

Social Identity Complexity

To explore why people might respond differently, I draw on Roccas and Brewer's (2002) theory of social identity complexity.

The framework describes four ways individuals represent their multiple social group memberships.

  • Intersection · A person defines their ingroup through the overlap of several group memberships.
  • Dominance · One group identity is more important than the others when defining who belongs to the ingroup.
  • Compartmentalization · Different group identities become important in different situations.
  • Merger · Several distinct group identities are recognised as parts of a broader, more inclusive understanding of ingroup membership.

How I Use These Four Patterns

I propose that these different ways of organising social identities may be related to how strongly individuals rely on group norms when making judgments.

My hypothesis is that individuals showing Intersection, Dominance, or Compartmentalization patterns may be more likely to rely on group-based judgments in certain situations.

By contrast, individuals showing a Merger pattern may be more willing to maintain judgments that differ from the position of a particular group.

This is my proposed theoretical interpretation, not an established conclusion of social identity complexity theory.

The four patterns provide a basis for comparing participants. The experiment is designed to explore whether their responses to conflicting group norms actually differ.

04 / PROPOSED ATTITUDE INTEGRATION MODEL

To explain how identity might influence attitude change, I propose a simple conceptual model.

When evaluating a person or group, individuals may consider two sources of information.

Group-Based Attitude: A_g(y)

What does my group think about this person?

For example: “Most people in my gender group dislike this type of person.”

Personal Attitude: A_p(y)

What do I think about this person based on my own experience?

For example: “I spoke with this person and found them friendly and reasonable.”

Both attitudes may influence the individual's final judgment. However, they may not have equal importance.

I therefore introduce two conceptual weights:

  • w_g: The importance given to group identity and group-based judgments.
  • w_p: The importance given to personal identity and experience-based judgments.

My hypothesis is:

When w_g is greater than w_p, individuals may be more likely to change their personal judgment to match their group.

When w_p is greater than w_g, individuals may be more likely to maintain their personal judgment despite group disagreement.

This model is a proposed explanation of attitude change. The weights have not yet been measured or empirically tested.

Importantly, the four social identity patterns do not automatically determine these weights. The relationship between identity structure and the relative importance of group and personal judgments is itself part of the proposed research.

05 / PROPOSED RESEARCH DESIGN

The main part of this project is a research design that creates a conflict between personal experience and group norms, then observes how people respond.

The design combines an initial survey with a two-stage experimental task.

Step 1: Identify Participants' Identity Structures

I propose recruiting approximately 100 participants, including 50 men and 50 women.

The survey would examine how participants understand their social identities, using measures informed by social identity complexity theory.

The aim is to identify participants representing four identity patterns: Intersection, Dominance, Compartmentalization, and Merger.

The survey would also explore how much importance participants attach to group membership and personal judgments.

Next, I would select a smaller sample for the experiment. For example, selecting five men and five women from each identity pattern would produce an initial experimental sample of approximately 40 participants.

Step 2: Create a Personal Judgment (First Evaluation, T1)

Each participant would encounter a short scripted interaction with a person of another gender.

The actor would express a relatively moderate opinion that might be considered unconventional within their own gender group.

After reading or participating in the interaction, participants would evaluate the actor. This is the first evaluation, T1.

At this stage, participants have not yet received information about how their own gender group evaluates this kind of person. The purpose is to observe their initial, experience-based judgment.

Step 3: Introduce a Conflicting Group Norm

Only participants who gave the actor a positive evaluation in T1 would continue to this stage.

These participants would receive information suggesting that most members of their own gender group evaluate this type of person negatively.

The experiment deliberately introduces a situation in which personal judgment and perceived group norms disagree. This is the cognitive conflict I want to investigate.

Step 4: Measure Attitudes Again (Second Evaluation, T2)

After receiving the negative group-norm information, participants would evaluate the same actor again using the same attitude questions. This provides a second measurement, T2.

Participants would also answer questions about their reasoning, such as whether the group information changed their opinion or whether they questioned the group's judgment.

Step 5: Compare What Happened

Imagine two participants who both rated the actor positively in the first evaluation and then received the same negative group-norm information.

Participant A: 6/7 before group-norm information, 3/7 after it. This pattern would be consistent with a shift toward the group's position.

Participant B: 6/7 before group-norm information, 6/7 after it. If their follow-up answers also show that they actively questioned the group's opinion, this would be consistent with independent reconsideration.

These numerical examples are illustrative, not actual results or a finalised measurement scale.

I would then examine whether these responses differ across the four identity patterns identified in Step 1, and whether the importance participants give to group membership helps explain these differences.

The four identity patterns are used to classify and compare participants. The experimental manipulation is the negative group-norm information.

06 / PROPOSED ANALYSIS

The main outcome would be the difference between the first and second evaluations.

Attitude Change = T2 − T1

A negative result means the participant's evaluation became less positive after receiving the group-norm information. A result close to zero means the evaluation remained relatively stable.

The analysis would compare these changes across the four identity patterns and examine their relationship with the relative importance of group-based and personal judgments.

I would also consider gender and participants' initial attitudes.

What I Expect to Find

My first hypothesis is that participants who rely more strongly on group-based judgments may show larger decreases in their positive evaluations after exposure to a negative group norm.

My second hypothesis is that participants who place greater importance on their personal experience may be more likely to maintain their initial evaluations.

I also expect that these tendencies may differ across the four social identity patterns.

However, maintaining an initial evaluation does not necessarily mean that someone has actively questioned the group norm. Participants might simply ignore the information or remain unaffected by it.

For this reason, their explanations and self-reported reasoning are important for distinguishing possible psychological responses.

The proposed study is exploratory. Its small experimental sample and the absence of a separate no-norm control condition would limit strong causal conclusions. The proposed identity weights would also require suitable measures before their explanatory role could be tested directly.

07 / RESEARCH SIGNIFICANCE

This research plan approaches intergroup tolerance through a specific psychological situation: what happens when a person's positive experience conflicts with the negative expectations of their own group?

The proposed contribution lies in the experimental design.

Instead of asking only whether individuals hold positive or negative attitudes toward another group, the study examines whether those attitudes change when conflicting group norms are introduced.

By measuring attitudes before and after the norm information, and comparing responses across different identity structures, the project seeks to explore the relationship between identity organisation, conformity, personal judgment, and intergroup tolerance.

The design also connects cognitive consistency theory with social identity complexity through a proposed attitude integration model.

Although the example focuses on gender-polarised debates in China, the same research question could be explored in other settings involving conflict between personal experience and group expectations.

Research status: This is a preliminary research plan developed as part of a university course. The survey and experimental procedures were proposed but not conducted. No empirical findings are claimed.
