Unmasking Digital Intimacy

A Critical Analysis of Emotion, Algorithms, and Economics
Digital Media, Culture and SocietyDigital Intimacy · Affective Computing · Emotional Capitalism

Lingji Yang · 2024

01 / Overview

As emotional experiences increasingly move into digital environments, artificial intelligence and interactive media are reshaping the ways people seek intimacy, companionship, and emotional fulfilment.

This paper critically examines the relationship between emotions, algorithms, and economic interests in the production of digital intimacy.

Drawing on literature from media studies, affect theory, and critical approaches to digital capitalism, it explores how emotionally responsive technologies can satisfy users' desires for companionship while also creating opportunities for emotional manipulation, data extraction, and commercial exploitation.

The paper argues that the apparent personalisation of digital intimacy often conceals the commercial systems through which emotional experiences are designed, circulated, and monetised.

02 / Theoretical Perspectives

The Affective Turn

Drawing on scholarship on the affective turn, the paper examines how emotion has become central to contemporary media technologies and digital economies.

Digital platforms increasingly treat emotional engagement as a valuable resource, using algorithmic systems to capture attention and sustain participation.

Digital Intimacy and Parasocial Relationships

The paper considers digital intimacy in relation to the transformation of traditional social relationships and the development of parasocial interaction.

Emotionally responsive technologies offer forms of closeness and companionship that may feel accessible, predictable, and less demanding than interpersonal relationships.

However, these experiences raise questions about reciprocity, authenticity, and the differences between simulated responsiveness and mutual emotional exchange.

Emotional Labour and Emotional Capitalism

Drawing on Arlie Russell Hochschild's concept of emotional labour and Eva Illouz's analysis of emotional capitalism, the paper explores how emotional experiences become intertwined with economic exchange.

Intimacy can become a commercial service, while users' emotional needs and personal disclosures acquire economic value.

03 / Cases from the Chinese Market

The paper examines three forms of commercially mediated intimacy.

Love and Deep Space

Love and Deep Space is an immersive romance simulation game that combines character narratives, visual realism, and interactive experiences.

The paper examines how its romantic scenarios and character relationships create a sense of personalised emotional attention within a commercially produced gaming environment.

Although players may experience these relationships as personally meaningful, the underlying romantic content is produced and distributed as a commercial product.

XingyeAI

XingyeAI represents a more adaptive form of digital companionship.

Unlike romance games that rely primarily on predetermined narratives, conversational AI generates responses based on users' inputs, creating experiences of individualised emotional interaction.

The paper argues that this responsiveness can also function as a form of emotional mirroring, in which users' disclosures become the material from which apparently personalised intimacy is constructed.

Cosplay Commissions

Cosplay commissions extend digital romantic experiences into embodied, paid interactions.

Users commission performers to portray fictional characters and participate in personalised romantic encounters.

This practice illustrates how attachment to digital characters can generate new forms of emotional labour and commercial exchange beyond the screen.

Together, these examples reveal different ways that intimacy is designed, personalised, performed, and monetised.

04 / Critical Analysis

Emotional AI as Self-Directed Performance

The paper argues that emotionally responsive AI can produce the appearance of reciprocal intimacy while organising interaction primarily around users' preferences and disclosures.

Rather than involving two independent participants negotiating a relationship, these systems generate responses through programmed mechanisms and user-provided information.

The resulting interaction can resemble a self-directed emotional performance reinforced through algorithmic feedback.

Social Withdrawal and Emotional Dependence

Drawing on Sherry Turkle and related scholarship, the paper considers how predictable, controllable digital companionship may encourage some users to retreat from the uncertainty of interpersonal relationships.

It critically examines the possibility that habitual reliance on simulated companionship could contribute to emotional dependence, self-oriented interaction, and social alienation.

These concerns are discussed as part of the paper's theoretical critique, rather than as experimentally established outcomes.

Users as Productive Consumers

A central argument concerns the dual position of users as producers and consumers of digital intimacy.

Through self-disclosure and ongoing interaction, users contribute the personal and emotional information that enables platforms to personalise their services.

They subsequently consume experiences constructed partly from their own emotional input.

Digital intimacy is therefore examined as a form of economic exchange in which emotional expression, personal data, and commercial value become increasingly interconnected.

05 / Conclusion

Digital intimacy offers new possibilities for companionship and emotional fulfilment, particularly in environments where users seek predictable and accessible emotional responses.

However, the paper argues that these possibilities cannot be separated from the commercial and technological structures through which intimate experiences are produced.

Emotional AI may simulate closeness without reproducing the reciprocity, unpredictability, and mutual vulnerability associated with interpersonal relationships.

The paper therefore advocates a critical understanding of emotionally responsive technologies and their economic incentives.

Rather than treating AI companionship as a replacement for human relationships, it proposes that emotional technologies should remain supportive tools that help users develop communication and emotional skills while preserving their capacity for meaningful interpersonal connection.
